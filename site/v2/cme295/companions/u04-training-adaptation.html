Unit 1 · C01

Pretraining: 6ND is the budget

From the lessonC01 items 2-5

Where does the base model's knowledge come from? The LM loss from U01, summed over trillions of tokens. No labels. The next token is the label. Pretraining is the act of reading the internet with a test after every word: predict the next one. The model that predicts well must compress grammar, facts, and reasoning patterns into its weights. Per token, the forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs and the backward pass about 4N, for 6ND total with N parameters and D tokens. Chinchilla's finding: the compute-optimal ratio is roughly D = 20 N. Train a 7B model on about 140B tokens, not 1T, at fixed compute.

From the lessonC01 item 6

The toy: N = 7e9, D = 1.4e11. FLOPs = 6 times 7e9 times 1.4e11 = 5.9e21. At 1e15 FLOP/s effective per GPU, that is 5.9e6 GPU-seconds, about 68 GPU-days on one card, or hours on thousands. The arithmetic is the budget. The training step itself is four lines: logits from the shifted batch, LM loss, backward, optimizer step. The shift by one is the whole trick.

From the lessonC01 items 4-5 Budget bench: 6ND FLOPs and GPU-days on your N, D, and throughput.

Reproduce the lesson: the defaults give 5.9e21 FLOPs and about 68 GPU-days. Try the Chinchilla check: D = 20 N.

From the lessonC01 item 11

More data does not always help. Duplicated or toxic data degrades the model. Quality beats quantity past a point. Deduplication and filtering are part of training, not decoration.

5.9e21FLOPs for 7B on 140B tokens
Nugget 01 · The arithmetic is the budget
The lesson

6ND FLOPs, balanced 20 to 1 by Chinchilla. Run the multiplication before you run the cluster.

What happened. The lesson derives 6ND from 2N forward plus 4N backward, states the 20:1 ratio, and prices 7B on 140B tokens at 5.9e21 FLOPs.

Why it matters. Lopsided scaling wastes the budget. Doubling compute at the Chinchilla ratio beats a param-heavy or data-heavy split. The ratio is a decision, not trivia.

Transferable principle. Price the work before you start it. One multiplication on a napkin beats a surprised invoice.

Unit 2 · C02

Pack the container, mask the loss

From the lessonC02 items 2-6

Documents have different lengths. How do you fill a batch without wasting half of it? Pack short documents end to end with a separator, and mask the loss so position i never trains on document j's tokens. The batch is a shipping container: pack it full, but label the boxes so the loss knows which is which. The toy: 4 documents of lengths [100, 300, 50, 550], T = 1024. Unpacked waste: 4 times 1024 minus 1000 = 3096 padding tokens, 75% waste. Packed: one 1000-token sequence, about 2% waste. The packing decision is a 75-point swing.

From the lessonC02 items 5, 9, 11

Without masking, padding tokens train the model to predict padding: the loss must zero them. Data quality is the manifest: dedupe, filter, mix. Dirty data wastes the most expensive resource, GPU time. And the mix is a hyperparameter with real effects: too much code starves prose fluency and vice versa.

75 ptsthe packing swing
Nugget 02 · The loss is only as honest as the manifest
The lesson

Pack every batch full, mask every padding token, and treat dedupe and filtering as training, not decoration.

What happened. The lesson computes the 75-point packing swing, derives the loss mask, and makes data quality a first-class pipeline stage.

Why it matters. Unmasked padding teaches the model to predict padding. Duplicated text gets memorized. Both are silent taxes on the most expensive resource you own.

Transferable principle. Efficiency and honesty are the same work. The mask that saves compute is the mask that keeps the objective true.

Unit 3 · C03

Quantization: a grid snapped over the numbers

From the lessonC03 items 2-6

The weights are fp16. Can they be int8 at half the memory? The toy: w = [-1.2, -0.3, 0.1, 0.7, 1.5]. Symmetric scale s = max|w| / 127, q = round(w / s). The scale sets the grid spacing, rounding snaps each value to the nearest grid point. Outliers stretch the grid and punish everyone. The lesson computes s = 0.01181, q = [-102, -25, 8, 59, 127], max abs error 0.00551, within the s/2 bound of 0.00591, as theory says. Dequantize: q times s.

From the lessonC03 items 9, 11

Memory halves from fp16 to int8, and memory-bound decode benefits immediately. Accuracy usually costs less than 1 point on benchmarks for weights-only. But one scale does not fit all: a single outlier channel stretches s and destroys the small weights. Per-channel scales or outlier-aware methods fix it.

From the lessonC03 items 4, 7 Quantizer bench: symmetric int8 on your weights.

Reproduce the lesson: the defaults give s = 0.01181 and max error 0.00551. Try the lab: -2, 0, 2.

s/2the quantization error bound
Nugget 03 · Outliers stretch the grid
The lesson

One scale, one rounding, error bounded by s/2. The grid is only as fine as the largest value allows.

What happened. The lesson writes the symmetric formulas, quantizes the toy by hand, and verifies the error bound.

Why it matters. The failure mode is distributional: one wild channel ruins the many. Per-channel scales are the standard fix, not a refinement.

Transferable principle. Global normalizations inherit the worst outlier. When one value sets the scale for all, check the tails first.

Unit 4 · C04

The roofline: intensity decides the regime

From the lessonC04 items 2-6

Decode does few FLOPs but takes forever. Training does many FLOPs and flies. Why? The toy: decode loads 14 GB of weights to do 1e9 FLOPs, intensity 0.07 FLOP/byte, memory-bound. Training reuses weights over big batches: compute-bound. Every kernel lives under a roof with two slopes: memory bandwidth and peak compute. Arithmetic intensity, FLOPs per byte, decides which slope binds. Time = max(bytes/bandwidth, FLOPs/peak).

From the lessonC04 item 6

The toy chip: bandwidth 2 TB/s, peak 300 TFLOP/s, ridge at 150 FLOP/byte. Decode at I = 0.07 reaches 0.14 TFLOP/s effective, 0.05% of peak. Training at I = 200 reaches the full 300 TFLOP/s. Same chip, 2000x different efficiency. That is the whole serving problem in one ratio.

From the lessonC04 items 4, 7 Roofline bench: attainable throughput on your FLOPs, bytes, bandwidth, peak.

Reproduce the lesson: the defaults give I = 0.07 and 0.05% MFU. Then try the lab: 1e12 FLOPs, 5e11 bytes.

From the lessonC04 item 11

FLOP counts do not predict serving cost. The decode toy at 0.05% MFU makes FLOP-based pricing nonsense. Always roofline before budgeting.

0.05%decode MFU vs training peak
Nugget 04 · Budget bytes, not FLOPs
The lesson

Below the ridge you wait on memory. Above it you wait on math. Decode lives far below, at five hundredths of a percent of peak.

What happened. The lesson derives intensity, draws the roofline, and prices decode at 0.05% MFU against training at full peak.

Why it matters. Quantization and MQA/GQA attack bytes, not math, because bytes are the binding slope. Optimizing FLOPs for a memory-bound workload is theater.

Transferable principle. Find the binding constraint before you optimize. The roofline is one division that prevents a quarter of wasted effort.

Unit 5 · C05

SFT: apprenticeship, not education

From the lessonC05 items 2-6

The base model completes text. How do you make it follow instructions? The toy pair: Summarize: doc to summary. Train the LM loss on the summary tokens only. SFT is apprenticeship: show the model thousands of (instruction, response) pairs. It learns the format (how to answer), not new facts (what to say). The base model holds the knowledge. SFT teaches manners. Masking the prompt means no gradient for predicting the instruction, which is given. The response tokens train normally.

From the lessonC05 items 6, 9

The toy budget: 10k pairs at mean 200 response tokens = 2M training tokens. At 6ND with N = 7e9: 8.4e16 FLOPs, minutes on a cluster. SFT costs little compared with pretraining. That is why everyone does it. Overfitting is the risk: few epochs, low learning rate. The lab variant: 50k pairs at 300 tokens = 15M tokens.

From the lessonC05 item 11

SFT does not teach facts. SFT on false answers teaches confident falsehoods. The base model's knowledge bounds what SFT can elicit. SFT elicits. It does not create.

8.4e16FLOPs for 2M SFT tokens
Nugget 05 · Elicit, do not expect creation
The lesson

SFT installs the format on masked responses. Knowledge comes from the base. Confident falsehoods come from SFT on falsehoods.

What happened. The lesson writes the masked SFT loss, budgets 2M tokens at 8.4e16 FLOPs, and draws the format-vs-fact line.

Why it matters. Blaming SFT for missing knowledge misdiagnoses the system. The base sets the ceiling. SFT decides which room you enter.

Transferable principle. Separate the channel from the content. Training the format cannot create the facts. Audit which one your problem actually lacks.

Unit 6 · C06

Chat templates: punctuation for dialogue

From the lessonC06 items 2-6

How does the model know where the user ends and the assistant begins? The toy: user marker, Hello, end marker, assistant marker, Hi, end marker. The markers are ordinary tokens with agreed meanings. The template is punctuation for dialogue. Special tokens are reserved ids added to the vocabulary: toy vocab 32000 + 3 = 32003 rows in E. Template overhead per turn: about 10 tokens. On a 20-turn conversation: 200 template tokens of pure format.

From the lessonC06 items 5, 9, 11

Train and serve must use the identical template, or the model reads a foreign format. At inference the prompt ends with the assistant marker and the model continues. The failure is silent and total: serve a Llama-format prompt to a Mistral-tuned model and behavior collapses, because the markers are unknown strings. Template bugs are silent quality killers. Unit-test the exact strings.

200format tokens per 20 turns
Nugget 06 · Byte-match the contract
The lesson

The template is a format contract between training and serving. Any deviation is a foreign language to the model.

What happened. The lesson defines markers, prices 200 format tokens per 20 turns, and shows the cross-model mismatch collapse.

Why it matters. Negligible compute, maximum brittleness. The cheapest part of the system causes the most confusing failures, because nothing errors. Quality just dies.

Transferable principle. Contracts that fail silently need tests, not trust. Where no error is raised, write the assertion yourself.

Unit 7 · C07

LoRA: the update lives in a thin subspace

From the lessonC07 items 2-6

Full fine-tuning moves 7B weights. What if the update is low-rank? The toy: W0 (8x8) frozen, B (8x2), A (2x8) trainable: 32 params instead of 64. The update is dW = BA. B and A are two thin factors whose product is the change. The base stays frozen. The adapter is the diff. Merge at deploy: W = W0 + BA. The alpha/r scale keeps the update magnitude stable as r changes. Initialize A small and B = 0, so the adapter starts as the identity and the model does not jump at step 0.

From the lessonC07 item 6

The lesson computes d = 4096, r = 8: full 16,777,216 vs LoRA 65,536, ratio 256. Rank of BA is at most r by construction. Forward adds O(r d) per token, negligible. Rank is the capacity knob. The lab asks for d = 1024, r = 16: 32,768 params.

From the lessonC07 item 11

Low rank does not always suffice. Tasks needing genuinely new capabilities, a new language or modality, exceed rank r. The adapter saturates and full tuning wins.

1/256LoRA params vs full
Nugget 07 · Thin diffs, frozen base
The lesson

Two thin factors carry the change at 1/256 the parameters. Start them at zero so day one changes nothing.

What happened. The lesson writes dW = BA, counts 65,536 vs 16,777,216, and justifies the alpha/r scale and the zero init.

Why it matters. The init trick is the whole safety story: B = 0 means the adapter is the identity at step 0. No jump, no surprise, then learning.

Transferable principle. Start every delta at zero. A change that begins as nothing can only earn its keep.

Unit 8 · C08

The Adam bill: freezing deletes two thirds

From the lessonC08 items 2-6

Where exactly do the 104 GB go in full fp32 Adam on 7B? Weights 28 GB (fp32), grads 28 GB, m+v 56 GB. Adam triples the bill: weights plus grads plus two states. Freezing the base deletes grads and states for 99.6% of parameters. The lesson computes: full fp32 Adam on 7B = 104.3 GiB, frozen bf16 base + LoRA (0.4% of weights trainable) = 13.4 GiB. That moves 7B training from 8xA100 to 1xA100 territory.

Illustration Honesty note, from the lesson itself. The lesson's lab toy uses a smaller adapter fraction, 0.06%, and prints 13.1 GiB. The two toys state different assumptions and both are correct under their own. This page charts the 0.4% / 13.4 GiB version from the lesson's render script.

From the lessonC08 items 4, 7 Memory bill bench: the lesson's three-term formula on your params.

Full fp32 Adam: 16 bytes/param. Frozen bf16 base: 2 bytes/param. The lab asks for 1B full fp32: 14.9 GiB.

From the lessonC08 item 11

The bill does not cover everything. Activations at long context can exceed the weights. The bill needs an activation term past T = 4k.

104→13GiB, the memory bill
Nugget 08 · The bill decides fit or no-fit
The lesson

Run the three-term memory bill before every launch. Freezing is the biggest win. Everything else is refinement.

What happened. The lesson names the three terms, computes 104.3 vs 13.4 GiB, and discloses the lab's differing assumption on-page.

Why it matters. The bill is the fit/no-fit decision for the GPU. Two toys, two adapter fractions, two honest numbers. Assumptions are part of the answer.

Transferable principle. State the assumptions with the number. A figure without its assumptions is a rumor with decimals.

Unit 9 · C09

Precision: range kills, precision blurs

From the lessonC09 items 2-6

fp16 halves memory. Why did early fp16 training diverge? The toy: gradient 1e-8 in fp16, whose min normal is about 6e-5. It rounds to zero. The update vanishes. fp16: 1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 mantissa bits, range about 6e-5 to 6e4. bf16: 1/8/7, range about 1e-38 to 3e38, like fp32. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by S, computes in fp16, then unscales gradients in fp32. The toy: true gradient 3e-6. fp16: 0, underflow. With loss scale 1024: 3e-3, representable, unscale recovers 3e-6. bf16: 3e-6 representable directly. bf16 needs no scaling because its range covers the gradients.

From the lessonC09 items 3-4 The three formats.
FormatBitsRangeNeeds scaling
fp321/8/23~1e-38 to 3e38no, the safe default
fp161/5/10~6e-5 to 6e4yes, loss scaling
bf161/8/7~1e-38 to 3e38no, the modern default

From the lessonC09 items 4, 11

Keep master weights in fp32, or the optimizer rounds updates to zero. And less precision is not always safe: fp16 without loss scaling on a deep net underflows gradients, and the model trains on noise. Precision is a budget, not a free lunch. Choose bf16 for training, fp16 or int8 for inference per hardware.

3e-6the gradient bf16 can see
Nugget 09 · Match the range to the signal
The lesson

Gradients live near zero. fp16 cannot see them without scaling. bf16 can. Pick the format whose range covers your smallest signal.

What happened. The lesson tabulates the three formats, derives loss scaling, and shows the 3e-6 gradient surviving in bf16 and scaled fp16.

Why it matters. Underflow is silent. The loss looks alive while the updates are zero. The mantissa difference rarely matters. The range difference decides.

Transferable principle. Resolution and range are different budgets. When small signals vanish, suspect range first.

Unit 10 · C10

Full vs PEFT: choose by depth of change

From the lessonC10 items 2-6

LoRA is cheap. Full tuning is free. When is cheap wrong? The toy: adapting to a new language needs deep change, a new format needs surface change. Rank 8 cannot hold a language. Full tuning: every weight may move, maximum freedom, maximum bill, maximum forgetting risk. PEFT: a thin diff on a frozen base, swappable, composable, bounded. The lesson prices it: full moves 16.8M params per matrix-class vs LoRA 65k, ratio 256. Adam 104.3 GiB vs 13.4 GiB. Merging is exact: W = W0 + BA, four lines, zero-latency deploy. Unmerge by subtracting.

From the lessonC10 item 11

PEFT does not guarantee no forgetting. A rank-64 adapter on a small base can still distort core behavior, and full tuning with good data and low LR forgets little. Both need evals. The lesson's chapter plate u04_fig04 shows the same tradeoff.

1 knobrank is the capacity dial
Nugget 10 · Depth of change decides
The lesson

New format: adapter. New language: full tuning. The rank is a capacity knob, and some changes exceed every rank.

What happened. The lesson trades the two strategies, prices both, and gives the exact merge.

Why it matters. Cost alone is the wrong chooser. The question is how much must change. A saturated adapter is just an expensive way to learn that rank was too small.

Transferable principle. Match the tool's capacity to the change's depth. Cheap tools fail expensively when the job exceeds them.

Unit 11 · C11

Canaries: a contract with the past

From the lessonC11 items 2-6

The fine-tune improved the task but broke refusals. How would you know before shipping? The toy: 50 canary prompts, 20 capability, 20 safety, 10 format. Gate: no canary regresses beyond tolerance. Before tune: record canary scores. After: re-run. Any drop beyond noise is a regression. Safety canaries (refusals, PII) are zero-tolerance. The toy verdict: capability 19/20 to 19/20, pass. Safety 20/20 to 18/20, FAIL: two refusals broke. The tune ships only after the safety fix. The numbers are the gate.

From the lessonC11 item 11

Canaries do not cover everything. A regression outside the canary set ships silently. Canaries need periodic renewal from real failures. And if canaries change with the tune, they are not canaries.

FAILthe toy gate failed on safety
Nugget 11 · Freeze the promises
The lesson

Every tune re-passes the old promises: answers stay right, refusals stay firm, format stays valid. Safety is zero-tolerance.

What happened. The lesson designs the canary set, defines the gate, and runs the toy to a FAIL verdict on safety.

Why it matters. Improvement on the task is not improvement overall. Without the contract, every tune is a gamble with the product's past behavior.

Transferable principle. Pin the invariants before you change anything. Progress you cannot gate is just motion.

Unit 12 · C12

The budget sheet: name the binding row

From the lessonC12 items 2-6

Can we fine-tune 7B with LoRA on one A100? The toy sheet: weights 14 GB (bf16), cache and activations about 20 GB at T = 2048, adapter states about 1 GB. Fits in 40 GB. FLOPs: 2M SFT tokens times 6 times 7e9 = 8.4e16, about 1 hour at 50% MFU, cost about $2-3 on cloud. Full fine-tune of the same: memory 104 GB (needs 3+ GPUs), time similar, cost 3x. The sheet makes the LoRA decision obvious.

From the lessonC12 items 4, 11

The sheet has four rows: FLOPs, memory, time, money. Memory decides fit (yes/no). FLOPs decide time. Time times GPU price decides money. And MFU 30-50% is honest. 70% is a dream. The sheet with 70% underestimates time 2x and the deadline slips.

From the lessonC12 items 4, 7 Budget bench: time and cost on your FLOPs, GPUs, MFU, and price.

Peak 1.56e14 is an A100 at bf16. Try MFU 0.7 and watch the honest 0.4 disagree.

3×full tuning costs 3x the sheet
Nugget 12 · The binding row decides
The lesson

Four rows, one binding constraint. Change the plan until all four pass, and distrust any sheet that assumes 70% MFU.

What happened. The lesson builds the sheet, fits LoRA 7B on one A100, and prices full tuning at 3x.

Why it matters. The sheet costs nothing. Skipping it costs a crashed run or a surprise bill. The binding row is the plan. The others are sanity checks.

Transferable principle. Every plan has a binding constraint. Name it explicitly, or it will name itself at the worst moment.
